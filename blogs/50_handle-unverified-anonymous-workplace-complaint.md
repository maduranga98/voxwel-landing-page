An anonymous complaint may contain serious information but very little detail. The reporter may not identify witnesses, dates or documents. An investigation may find no independent evidence, or the available accounts may conflict. HR then faces two responsibilities: take the concern seriously and avoid treating an unproven allegation as an established fact.

**Direct answer:** Assess the complaint for specificity, urgency and testable facts. Make proportionate enquiries, preserve relevant evidence, interview appropriate people and document the limits of the investigation. If the evidence is insufficient, record the outcome as inconclusive or not substantiated under the organization's policy. Do not label the report malicious without evidence of intentional wrongdoing.

---

## 1. Identify what can be tested

Break the report into separate allegations. For each one, identify the alleged conduct, approximate date, location, people involved, possible witnesses and records that might exist. If the report is vague, decide whether a limited preliminary review can clarify the issue without exposing unnecessary personal data.

---

## 2. Assess immediate risk

Even if details are incomplete, a report may indicate a risk to health, safety, finances, data or the integrity of a process. Consider whether proportionate interim steps are needed while facts are checked. Any measures should be based on risk and should not function as punishment before a finding.

---

## 3. Look for independent information

Review records that are directly relevant to the allegation. Interview witnesses using neutral questions. Avoid asking employees to guess who submitted an anonymous report or to conduct their own informal investigation. Keep the scope proportionate and respect privacy rules. See [how to investigate anonymous workplace complaints](/blogs/investigate-anonymous-workplace-complaints).

---

## 4. Give affected people a fair process

Where appropriate, the person accused should have a meaningful opportunity to respond to the substance of the concern. Do not disclose the reporter's identity or unnecessary identifying details. Explain that the organization is assessing information, not making a conclusion in advance.

---

## 5. Distinguish the possible outcomes

A practical outcome framework may include:

- **Substantiated:** available evidence supports the allegation under the organization's standard of assessment.
- **Partially substantiated:** some elements are supported, while others are not.
- **Not substantiated:** the evidence does not support the allegation to the required level.
- **Inconclusive:** there is not enough reliable information to reach a conclusion.
- **Outside scope or referred:** another process or authority is responsible.

The exact labels and standards should follow the organization's policy and applicable law. A report that is not substantiated is not automatically a knowingly false report. A person may have reported a concern honestly but lacked the evidence needed to establish it.

---

## 6. Consider whether a broader control issue remains

An allegation may be difficult to prove but still point to a weak approval process, inadequate supervision, unclear policy or missing reporting route. Consider whether a process review or preventive measure is justified independently of the individual allegation.

---

## 7. Close the loop carefully

Record the steps taken, the material considered, the limitations and the reason for the outcome. Provide appropriate feedback without revealing private personnel information. Where possible, invite further information through the reporting channel, but do not imply that an anonymous reply is available unless the channel actually supports it.

---

## What not to do

- Do not dismiss an allegation solely because it is anonymous.
- Do not treat anonymity as evidence of bad faith.
- Do not circulate the report widely to find the reporter.
- Do not discipline the person accused based only on an untested allegation.
- Do not discipline the reporter simply because the investigation was inconclusive.

A fair process protects everyone involved. It allows the organization to act on credible information while recognizing the difference between an allegation, the evidence and a finding. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can an organization close an anonymous complaint as inconclusive?
Yes, where reasonable enquiries have been made and the evidence is insufficient to reach a reliable conclusion. The rationale and steps taken should be documented.

### Is an unsubstantiated complaint a false complaint?
No. "Unsubstantiated" describes the evidence outcome. A knowingly false report is a different question and should require evidence of intentional dishonesty.

### Should the accused employee be told who submitted the complaint?
Not automatically. Share only information that is necessary for a fair process and permitted by applicable law. Protect the reporter's identity and third-party information to the extent possible.
