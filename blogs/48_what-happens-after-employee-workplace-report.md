Submitting a workplace concern can feel like sending a message into a locked room. Employees want to know whether anyone will read it, whether they will be contacted and whether the issue will be taken seriously. Employers need a process that answers those questions without compromising confidentiality or the fairness of an investigation.

**Direct answer:** After a report is submitted, an organization should record receipt, acknowledge it where appropriate, assess urgency and scope, assign an impartial handler, decide what follow-up is needed, investigate fairly, communicate appropriate updates, take proportionate action and document closure. The exact steps and deadlines depend on the policy and applicable law.

---

## Step 1: The report is received and recorded

The reporting channel should capture the information needed to understand the concern and record when it arrived. It should not request unnecessary personal information. The organization should explain who receives reports and how the information will be used.

---

## Step 2: The employee receives acknowledgement

Where the process and law require it, the organization should confirm receipt. Under Article 9 of the EU Whistleblowing Directive, internal channels must support acknowledgement within seven days, subject to the conditions in the Directive. An acknowledgement should confirm receipt, explain the next step and avoid promising an outcome before the facts are reviewed.

---

## Step 3: The concern is assessed

A trained person considers whether there is an immediate safety risk, an ongoing problem, a conflict of interest or a need to preserve evidence. The report may be assigned to HR, Compliance, Legal, Health and Safety or an independent investigator depending on its subject matter.

If the report names the normal recipient, it should be routed away from that person. The accused person should not be able to access or control the report through ordinary administrator permissions. See [who investigates when HR or senior management is accused](/blogs/who-investigates-complaints-against-hr-or-senior-management).

---

## Step 4: The organization decides how to investigate

Some issues can be addressed through a policy explanation or management intervention. Serious allegations may require interviews, document review, system records or external expertise. The investigation should be proportionate to the allegation and conducted by someone who can be impartial.

The organization should separate allegations from findings. A report is information to assess, not proof that misconduct occurred.

---

## Step 5: The reporter receives appropriate updates

Employees should know when they can expect an update and how to request one. For anonymous reporters, the channel needs a workable communication route if the organization intends to provide updates without identifying them. Do not promise two-way communication unless the process actually supports it.

The organization may need to limit the details it shares because the case involves other employees, personal data, legal rights or an ongoing investigation. A meaningful update can still explain whether the matter is being reviewed, whether more information is needed and when the next update is expected.

---

## Step 6: The organization takes action and closes the case

If the evidence supports a concern, the organization should take appropriate corrective or preventive action. This might include changing a process, improving controls, providing training or applying disciplinary measures consistent with policy and law. If the evidence is insufficient, the organization should record the limits of the investigation and any broader lessons.

Closure should include a documented decision, outstanding actions, required notifications and a retention review. A case should not remain open indefinitely because no one owns the final step.

---

## What employees can reasonably expect

Employees should expect their report to be handled under a defined process, access to be restricted and retaliation concerns to be taken seriously. They should not expect every detail of an investigation or another person's disciplinary outcome to be disclosed. The organization should explain these boundaries before reports are submitted.

A reporting process earns trust through consistent follow-up. The most useful proof is not a slogan on a website. It is what the organization does after someone speaks up. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to investigate a workplace complaint?
There is no universal timeline. It depends on the seriousness, evidence, number of witnesses, legal requirements and complexity. Organizations should set realistic updates and avoid unexplained silence.

### Will HR tell the employee the result?
The organization should provide appropriate feedback under its policy and applicable law, but it may not be able to disclose confidential personnel details or every investigative finding.

### Can an anonymous employee receive updates?
Only if the reporting process provides a way to communicate without revealing identity. Employers should test and clearly explain this capability.
