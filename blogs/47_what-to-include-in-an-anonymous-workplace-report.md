An anonymous workplace report is most useful when it gives the organization enough information to understand what happened and decide what to check next. Employees do not need to write like lawyers or investigators. Clear, specific facts are more helpful than a long account filled with assumptions.

**Direct answer:** Include what happened, when and where it happened, who may have been involved, how you know about it, whether anyone is at immediate risk and what records or witnesses may help verify the concern. Separate what you personally saw from what someone told you or what you suspect. Do not include unnecessary personal information.

---

## 1. Describe the conduct, not just the label

Instead of writing only "my manager is corrupt" or "the team is being harassed", describe the actions that led you to that conclusion. For example, explain that a manager approved an invoice for a supplier after the required review was skipped, or describe the words or conduct that made a colleague feel unsafe.

Use neutral language. Avoid guessing about motives or making claims you cannot support. The investigator can assess whether the facts may indicate fraud, harassment, discrimination, retaliation, safety concerns or another policy breach.

---

## 2. Include dates, times and locations

Give the closest date or time you can remember. If you do not know the exact date, provide a range or connect the event to something identifiable, such as a shift, meeting, project milestone or payment cycle. Include the relevant office, site, department or online workspace.

A timeline helps investigators find records and identify people who may have relevant information. If the conduct happened repeatedly, list examples rather than writing only "this happens all the time".

---

## 3. Identify people who may have relevant information

Name the people involved only where you know their role or identity and it is relevant. Distinguish the person whose conduct is being reported from witnesses or people who may hold useful records. Do not include unrelated personal details about colleagues.

If you do not know a name, describe the person's role, team, shift or other details that may help identify the relevant context.

---

## 4. Explain how you know

Say whether you personally witnessed the event, received the information from someone else, reviewed a document or noticed a pattern in records. This helps the investigator understand how direct the information is and what needs independent confirmation.

It is acceptable to say "I do not know" or "I heard this from another person". Clear uncertainty is more useful than presenting an assumption as a fact.

---

## 5. Point to evidence without taking unnecessary risks

Mention relevant documents, invoices, messages, schedules, system records, photographs or other evidence that may exist. Do not access systems you are not authorized to use, remove confidential business records unlawfully or put yourself at risk to obtain evidence. The organization can decide what information it may lawfully preserve and review.

Before attaching a file, consider whether it contains personal information about other people or hidden metadata. Follow the reporting channel's instructions and do not upload unrelated material.

---

## 6. Explain whether the issue is ongoing or urgent

Tell the recipient whether the conduct is continuing, whether someone may be harmed soon, whether evidence may disappear or whether there is a risk of retaliation. Immediate threats to safety may require an urgent route outside an ordinary reporting queue, depending on the situation and local emergency procedures.

---

## 7. Say what outcome or follow-up would help

Where possible, explain what you want the organization to review or what change may prevent the issue from happening again. You do not need to decide the disciplinary outcome. You can ask for the issue to be investigated, a safety risk to be addressed or a process to be reviewed.

---

## A simple report-writing structure

A useful report can follow this structure: *"I am reporting [specific conduct]. It happened around [date/time] at [location or team]. I know about it because [first-hand observation or source]. The people or records that may help are [relevant details]. The issue is [ongoing/not ongoing/urgent]. I am concerned because [impact or risk]."*

Only include details you know or reasonably believe to be relevant. If a reporting form does not ask every question, provide the most useful information you can without trying to investigate the matter yourself.

---

## Protecting your identity

If you are choosing an anonymous channel, read its privacy explanation. Check whether it requires a named employee login, whether it offers follow-up without revealing your identity and who can access submitted information. Remember that the facts you describe may indirectly identify you, even when you do not enter your name.

A well-written report does not need to prove misconduct. Its purpose is to give the organization a fair starting point for checking the concern. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need evidence before submitting an anonymous report?
No. Share the facts you know and identify records or witnesses that may help. Do not try to obtain evidence through unauthorized access or risky actions.

### Should I include the names of witnesses?
Where relevant, identify people who may have direct information. Avoid including unrelated personal details or asking others to coordinate accounts.

### What if I do not remember the exact date?
Give an approximate date, time range or related event. Be clear that the detail is approximate rather than guessing.
